What a quantum clock does to a type III algebra

Every local algebra of a reasonable QFT is the same type III1 factor: no trace, no density matrices, no finite entanglement entropy. Chandrasekaran, Longo, Penington and Witten found that adding an observer to the de Sitter static patch turns the algebra into type II1. This paper explains why in operational terms. On a background with symmetries you cannot even say which experiment you did without a reference frame. Make that frame quantum, keep only symmetry-invariant observables, and the algebra changes type.

This guide builds on the measurement framework of Fewster and Verch (2020); the reading guide to that paper covers it in detail. Hover over any coloured symbol or dotted term for its definition, or open the Notation panel. Section, equation and theorem numbers refer to the paper.

01Paper §1

The question

In algebraic QFT, the observables localised in a region form a von Neumann algebra. A striking structural result says these are all the same: for well-behaved theories and regular regions, every local algebra is the unique hyperfinite factor of type III1. What distinguishes one QFT from another is how the algebras of different regions sit relative to each other, not the algebras themselves.

Type III1 has a practical cost. Such an algebra has no trace, so states cannot be written as density matrices and entropies cannot be computed; naive attempts diverge. Chandrasekaran, Longo, Penington and Witten (CLPW, 2022) argued that in the de Sitter static patch, once an observer is included and observables are required to be invariant under the patch's time translations, the algebra becomes type II1. A type II1 algebra has a finite trace, and entropies make sense.

CLPW model the observer as a quantum system with a Hamiltonian but do not say how it is coupled to the field. This paper supplies an operational account. Its claims:

  • Measuring a field on a background with symmetries requires a reference frame. This follows from the Fewster–Verch measurement framework.
  • CLPW's observer is a quantum reference frame (QRF).
  • Type reduction comes from including the frame and keeping only invariant observables. It is not directly gravitational in origin.
  • The result holds for a large class of frames and symmetry groups ℝ × H, with a clean condition on the frame.

The argument needs four toolkits: measurement theory in QFT, quantum reference frames, crossed products, and Tomita–Takesaki modular theory. The sections below introduce each where it is first used.

02Paper App. D · Defs D.4–D.8

Types of von Neumann algebras

A von Neumann algebra is a *-algebra of bounded operators equal to its double commutant. A factor has trivial centre; every von Neumann algebra on a separable Hilbert space decomposes into factors. Factors are classified by the traces they carry. A trace is a weight \(\tr:\mathcal M_+\to[0,\infty]\) with \(\tr(x^*x)=\tr(xx^*)\). The algebra is finite if it has a faithful normal trace with \(\tr(\one)<\infty\), and semifinite if every nonzero positive element dominates one of finite trace. On a semifinite factor the trace is unique up to scale, so the set of values it takes on projections is an invariant.

Two facts from modular theory drive the paper. Takesaki showed that the crossed product of any von Neumann algebra by its modular flow is semifinite, and that for a type III1 factor it is a type II∞ factor Thm 5.3 · Prop D.9. A corner \(p\,\mathcal N p\) of a II∞ factor 𝒩 is again a factor, and it is type II1 exactly when \(\tr(p)<\infty\). The paper's job is to show that a quantum reference frame produces such a corner, and to compute \(\tr(p)\).

03Paper §2.2–2.3 · Thm 2.1

Measuring on a symmetric background

Recall the Fewster–Verch scheme (guide). A system QFT \(\mathcal A\) is coupled to a probe QFT \(\mathcal B\) inside a compact region \(\K\). Preparing the probe in a state \(\sigma\) and reading a probe observable \(B\) late amounts to measuring the induced system observable \(\varepsilon_\sigma(B)=\eta_\sigma(\Theta(\one\otimes B))\). The whole package is a measurement scheme \(m=(\mathcal B,\mathcal C,\K,\chi,\sigma,B)\).

Now let a group \(G\) of isometries act on spacetime, and on both theories by automorphisms \(\alpha(g)\), \(\beta(g)\), commuting with gauge symmetries. Moving the coupling region by \(g\) gives a new coupled theory \({}^g\mathcal C\) with coupling region \(g.\K\) and scattering map \(\upsilon(g)\circ\Theta\circ\upsilon(g)^{-1}\), where \(\upsilon=\alpha\otimes\beta\).

Theorem 2.1 If \((\mathcal B,\mathcal C,\K,\chi,\sigma,B)\) is a measurement scheme for \(A\), then \((\mathcal B,{}^g\mathcal C,g.\K,{}^g\chi,\beta(g^{-1})^*\sigma,\beta(g)B)\) is a measurement scheme for \(\alpha(g)A\).

Any single scheme breaks the symmetry, because it has a definite coupling region. But \(G\) acts on the set of all schemes, and the induced observables transform covariantly. In general relativity points have no meaning of their own, so neither does "the coupling region is here". On a fixed background only isometries matter, but the conclusion is the same: \(m\) and \(g.m\) cannot be told apart by anything intrinsic. Distinguishing them requires a physical system that itself transforms under \(G\). If that system is quantum, it is a quantum reference frame.

The paper's main example is the static patch of de Sitter space: the causal hull of an observer's complete timelike geodesic, the largest region in which that observer can both start an experiment and hear its result. Its isometry group is \(G=\mathbb R\times SO(n-1)\): time translations along the geodesic (the static flow) and rotations about it.

04Paper §3 · Defs 3.1, 3.2 · Thm 3.3

Quantum reference frames

The paper follows the operational approach to QRFs (Loveridge, Miyadera, Busch and others), in which observables are POVMs. A value space \(\Sig\) is a homogeneous \(G\)-space, so \(\Sig\cong G/H\) for the stabiliser \(H\) of any point.

Definition 3.1 A quantum reference frame \((\UR,\E,\HR)\) is a strongly continuous unitary representation \(\UR\) of \(G\) on \(\HR\) together with a POVM \(\E:\mathrm{Bor}(\Sig)\to\Br(\HR)\) that is covariant: \[ \E(g.X)=\UR(g)\,\E(X)\,\UR(g)^*\qquad\text{for all }g\in G,\ X\in\mathrm{Bor}(\Sig). \]

The frame observable \(\E\) answers "where is the frame?" with values in \(\Sig\), and the answer moves correctly when the frame is moved. Frames come with adjectives:

Sharp
\(\E\) is projection-valued. Otherwise unsharp.
Principal
\(\Sig\) is a principal homogeneous space, i.e. a copy of \(G\) itself.
Ideal
Principal and sharp.
Compactly stabilised
\(\Sig\cong G/H\) with \(H\) compact. The class that matters in this paper.
Localisable
Every nonzero \(\E(X)\) has norm 1: some state makes the frame's reading lie in \(X\) with probability as close to 1 as you like.
Complete
No nontrivial subgroup acts trivially on all the effects of \(\E\).

Examples: position of a particle on a line, covariant under translations (ideal); a smeared position (unsharp); the phase of an oscillator, covariant under phase shifts. The most important example is a clock.

Clocks with positive energy

A clock is a frame for time translations \(\mathbb R\), with \(\UR(t)=e^{iHt}\). Pauli's theorem says that if \(H\) is bounded below, no projection-valued measure \(P\) satisfies \(e^{itH}P(X)e^{-itH}=P(X+t)\). There is no self-adjoint time operator. Covariant POVMs do exist. CLPW's observer has Hamiltonian \(q_+\) on \(L^2(\mathbb R_+)\), and a covariant time observable is obtained by Naimark dilation Thm 3.3: embed \(L^2(\mathbb R_+)\) in \(L^2(\mathbb R)\) and compress the spectral measure of the variable conjugate to energy. In practice the time distribution of a clock state is \(|\check\psi(t)|^2\), the squared Fourier transform of its energy wavefunction.

The invariance principle

Given a system with von Neumann algebra \(\MS\) on which \(G\) acts by \(\aS\), the operational approach declares that the physical observables are the invariant elements of the joint algebra:

\[ \inv{\MS\otimes\Br(\HR)}=\{x\in\MS\otimes\Br(\HR):\ (\aS(g)\otimes\mathrm{Ad}\,\UR(g))(x)=x\ \ \forall g\}. \]

This is not only a postulate. A strong form of the Wigner–Araki–Yanase theorem says that if a system has a conserved quantity, all of its measurable observables must be invariant under the symmetry that quantity generates.

05Paper §3.2 · eq. (3.5)

Relative observables

For a principal frame there is a standard way to manufacture invariants from system observables, the relativisation map (written with a yen sign in this literature):

\[ \Yen(A)=\int_G \US(g)\,A\,\US(g)^*\otimes d\E(g). \]

Read it as "\(A\), as seen from wherever the frame is". It is completely positive, normal and unital, and it is multiplicative exactly when \(\E\) is sharp. On a line with \(G=\mathbb R\) acting by translations, the system's position \(Q_S\) relativises to the relative position \(Q_S\otimes\one-\one\otimes Q_R\).

How does this square with ordinary quantum mechanics, where absolute positions seem to work? Compose with the restriction map \(\etas(A\otimes B)=\sigma(B)A\), which fixes the frame in a state \(\sigma\). If the frame is localisable, there are frame states \(\sigma_n\) with \(\eta_{\sigma_n}\circ\Yen\to\mathrm{id}\): a well-localised frame at the origin reproduces the usual description. A badly localised frame cannot.

06Paper §4.1 · Def 4.1 · Props 4.2, 4.3 · eqs. (4.10)–(4.12)

A frame selects the experiment

An experimenter has a set \(\mathcal M\) of measurement schemes, closed under \(G\). Its orbits \([m]\in\mathcal M/G\) are the experiments that the background alone cannot tell apart. A frame with value space \(\Sig\) resolves \(\mathcal M\) if for every orbit some \(s\in\Sig\) and some \(m\in[m]\) have stabilisers \(G_s\subset G_m\). Equivalently (Prop. 4.2), there is a covariant labelling

\[ f:\Sig\times\mathcal M/G\to\mathcal M,\qquad f(s,[m])\in[m],\qquad f(g.s,[m])=g.f(s,[m]). \]

The procedure is then: read the frame, get a value \(s\), and perform the scheme \(f(s,[m])\). The reading is random, with distribution \(\mu_R=\omega_R\circ\E\), so the expected outcome is

\[ \mathbb E\bigl[\omega_S(A_{f(S,[m])})\bigr]=\int_{\Sig}\omega_S\bigl(A_{f(s,[m])}\bigr)\,d(\omega_R\circ\E)(s)=(\omega_S\otimes\omega_R)(A_{[m]}), \]

where \(A_{[m]}=\int A_{f(s,[m])}\otimes d\E(s)\) is a \(G\)-invariant operator in \(\inv{\MS\otimes\Br(\HR)}\). The paper makes this formula rigorous in §4.4 as a relativised observable.

Proposition 4.3 Suppose every nonempty compact \(\K\subset M\) has a compact stabiliser \(G_\K\), and \(\mathcal M\) consists of schemes with nonempty compact coupling regions. Then any frame that resolves \(\mathcal M\) is compactly stabilised.

WhyA symmetry fixing a scheme must fix its coupling region, so \(G_m\subset G_\K\). Resolution needs \(G_s\subset G_m\) for some \(s\), so \(G_s\) is a closed subgroup of a compact group. The hypothesis holds on the de Sitter static patch and the Schwarzschild exterior, whose isometry groups act properly.

07Paper §4.2–4.4 · Thms 4.4, 4.9 · Prop 4.8 · Def 4.13

Invariants are a crossed product

The first structural result says that every compactly stabilised frame is a piece of one standard frame. Let \(\lambda\) be the left regular representation of \(G\) on \(L^2(G)\) and \(P\) the obvious projection-valued measure on \(G/H\) (multiplication by indicator functions).

Theorem 4.4 If \((\UR,\E,\HR)\) is a compactly stabilised frame with value space \(G/H\), there is a separable Hilbert space \(\Kc\) and an isometry \(W:\HR\to\Kc\otimes L^2(G)\) with \[ W\,\UR(g)=(\one_{\Kc}\otimes\lambda(g))\,W,\qquad \E(X)=W^*(\one_{\Kc}\otimes P(X))\,W. \] Conversely, any isometry whose range projection commutes with \(\one\otimes\lambda(G)\) defines such a frame.

In wordsUp to equivalence a compactly stabilised frame is the regular representation (with multiplicity \(\Kc\)) restricted to an invariant subspace \(p(\Kc\otimes L^2(G))\). The proof combines Naimark dilation with Mackey's imprimitivity theorem, in Cattaneo's form for systems of covariance (App. B).

The invariant algebra then reduces to a classical object. The crossed product \(\mathcal M\cp_\alpha G\) acts on \(L^2(G,\mathcal H)\) and is generated by the operators \(\pi(a)\), \((\pi(a)\varphi)(g)=\alpha(g,a)\varphi(g)\), and the right translations \(\tilde\rho(G)\). Takesaki's commutation theorem identifies it as a fixed-point algebra Prop 4.8:

\[ \mathcal M\cp_\alpha G=\bigl(\mathcal M\otimes\Br(L^2(G))\bigr)^{\alpha\otimes\mathrm{Ad}\,\lambda}. \]
Theorem 4.9 For a compactly stabilised frame there are \(\Kc\) and a projection \(p\in\lambda(G)'\otimes\Br(\Kc)\) such that, spatially, \[ \inv{\MS\otimes\Br(\HR)}\;\cong\;(\one\otimes p)\bigl((\MS\cp_{\aS}G)\otimes\Br(\Kc)\bigr)(\one\otimes p). \]

The invariant observables are a corner of the crossed product, tensored with a type I factor. This generalises the parameterisation CLPW used for \(G=\mathbb R\) and \(\Kc=\mathbb C\).

Generator type 1System observables read at the clock's time. For an ideal clock on \(L^2(\mathbb R)\) with position \(\hat q\) and momentum \(\hat p\):
\(\Yen(a)=e^{iH_S\otimes\hat q}\,(a\otimes\one)\,e^{-iH_S\otimes\hat q}\), with \(a\in\MS\).
Generator type 2The clock's own energy, \(\one\otimes\hat p\), which commutes with the joint time translation.
Conjugating by \(e^{-iH_S\otimes\hat q}\) turns the pair into \(a\otimes\one\) and \(H_S\otimes\one+\one\otimes\hat p\).

So for \(G=\mathbb R\) the invariants are generated by the system's observables together with the total energy of system plus clock (signs depend on conventions). For a type III algebra the generator \(H_S\) of the symmetry is not in \(\MS\), so adding it changes the algebra; this is the form in which CLPW state their result. A clock with energy restricted to a set \(V\) cuts this down to a corner by the spectral projection \(p_V\) of \(\hat p\).

The relativisation map extends to compactly stabilised frames Def 4.13 · Prop 4.14: for \(H\)-invariant \(x\in\MS\), \(\Yen(x)=W^*(\pi(x)\otimes\one)W\). It is completely positive, unital and normal, and a *-isomorphism onto its image when \(\E\) is sharp. The invariant operators \(A_{[m]}=\Yen(A_{f(H,[m])})\) of the previous section are exactly of this form Cor 4.16. A further map \(Z\) (Cor. 4.11) parameterises all invariants, not just relativised ones.

08Paper §4.5 · Prop 4.17 · Def 4.18

How much energy a frame has

From here on \(G=\mathbb R\times H\) with \(H\) compact: time translations times something like rotations. Fourier transforming the \(L^2(\mathbb R)\) factor in Theorem 4.4 decomposes the frame's Hilbert space over the energy \(\xi\) conjugate to time:

\[ \HR\cong\int_{\mathbb R}^{\oplus}\Kc(\xi)\,d\xi,\qquad (U(t,h)\psi)(\xi)=e^{it\xi}\,U_H(\xi)(h)\,\psi(\xi). \]

The spectral multiplicity function \(\mU(\xi)=\dim\Kc(\xi)\) counts the frame's degrees of freedom at energy \(\xi\). Covariance forces the energy spectrum to be absolutely continuous, so \(\mU\) is a density, not a list of eigenvalues. For a clock on \(L^2(\mathbb R)\) with energy restricted to a set \(V\), \(\mU=\chi_V\). This single function is all of the frame that enters the main theorem.

09Paper §5 · Def 5.1 · Thms 5.2, 5.3 · Prop 5.4

Modular theory on one page

KMS states. A normal state \(\omega\) is KMS at inverse temperature \(\bet\) for a flow \(\alpha_t\) if for all \(x,y\) there is a bounded continuous function on the strip \(0\le\mathrm{Im}\,z\le\bet\), analytic inside, with boundary values

\[ F_{x,y}(t)=\omega\bigl(y\,\alpha_t(x)\bigr),\qquad F_{x,y}(t+i\bet)=\omega\bigl(\alpha_t(x)\,y\bigr). \]

For a finite system this is the Gibbs state \(e^{-\beta H}/Z\); the definition survives in infinite volume and for type III algebras, where density matrices do not.

Tomita–Takesaki. Any faithful normal state \(\omega\) on a von Neumann algebra \(\mathcal M\) has a modular flow. With \(\Omega\) its GNS vector, the closure of \(Sx\Omega=x^*\Omega\) has polar decomposition \(S=J\Del^{1/2}\), and \(\Mod_t(x)=\Del^{it}x\Del^{-it}\) maps \(\mathcal M\) to itself. The state is KMS for \(\Mod\) at inverse temperature \(-1\) Thm 5.2.

Geometric modular flow. If \(\omega\) is KMS at \(\bet\) for a flow \(\alpha_t\) coming from a spacetime symmetry, uniqueness gives \(\Mod_t=\alpha_{-\beta t}\). The abstract modular flow is then a time translation. On the de Sitter static patch, a state that is KMS for the static flow (the Bunch–Davies state, at the Gibbons–Hawking temperature, is the standard example) has exactly this property. The paper stresses that geometric modular action is rare and fragile, and that its results depend on it.

Takesaki's theorem. The modular crossed product \(\mathcal M\cp_{\Mod}\mathbb R\) has a faithful normal semifinite trace Thm 5.3. Up to spatial isomorphism it does not depend on the state. Concretely, on spectral projections \(p_K\) of the generator of the \(\mathbb R\)-translations (the "frame energy"),

\[ \tr(p_K)=\int_K e^{\xi}\,d\xi \qquad\text{(Prop. 5.4).} \]

Energies are weighted exponentially. The whole algebra has \(\tr(\one)=\int_{\mathbb R}e^{\xi}d\xi=\infty\), so it is semifinite but not finite. For a type III1 factor, \(\mathcal M\cp_{\Mod}\mathbb R\) is a type II∞ factor Prop D.9. Rescaling the time variable from modular to physical time turns \(e^{\xi}\) into \(\beta e^{-\beta\xi}\): the weight is a Boltzmann factor.

10Paper §5.1 · Thm 5.5 · Cor 5.7 · eqs. (5.41)–(5.43)

The main theorem

Theorem 5.5 Let \(G=\mathbb R\times H\) with \(H\) compact act on \(\MS\) by a strongly continuous unitary representation, and let \(\omega\) be a \(G\)-invariant faithful normal state that is KMS at inverse temperature \(\bet\in(0,\infty)\) for the \(\mathbb R\)-action. For every compactly stabilised frame \((\UR,\E,\HR)\), the invariant algebra \(\inv{\MS\otimes\Br(\HR)}\) is semifinite. If moreover \[ \int_{\mathbb R}e^{-\bet\xi}\,\mU(\xi)\,d\xi<\infty, \] then the invariant algebra is finite.
Corollary 5.7 If in addition \(\MS\) is a type III1 factor and \(H\) is trivial, the invariant algebra is a type II1 factor if and only if the integral above is finite.

Where the integral comes fromThe trace built from Takesaki's trace and the type I trace on \(\Br(\Kc)\) gives \(\tr(\one\otimes p)=\bet\int e^{-\bet\xi}\mU(\xi)\,d\xi\) (eq. 5.43). Each energy shell of the frame contributes its number of degrees of freedom times a Boltzmann factor. A frame with too much weight at low or negative energy, or too many states at high energy, makes the trace of the identity infinite.

For general \(H\) the condition is sufficient but not necessary, because the invariant algebra need not be a factor and so its trace is not unique Remark 5.6. Figure 7 evaluates the condition for the frames discussed in the paper and two more for comparison.

How the proof goes

  1. Naimark, Mackey, Cattaneo · Thm 4.4Put the frame inside the regular representation.\(\HR\cong p(\Kc\otimes L^2(G))\), with \(\UR\) the left translations and \(\E\) the compressed canonical PVM.
  2. Takesaki's commutation theorem · Thm 4.9Turn invariants into a crossed product.The invariants are \(p\bigl((\MS\cp G)\otimes\Br(\Kc)\bigr)p\).
  3. KMS ⇒ modular · eq. (5.23)Recognise the time translations as modular.With \(\omega\) KMS at \(\bet\), the \(\mathbb R\)-action is the modular flow up to rescaling, so \(\MS\cp\mathbb R\) is a modular crossed product.
  4. Takesaki · Thm 5.3, Prop 5.4Use its semifinite trace.Tensor with the trace on the type I factor \(\Br(L^2(H)\otimes\Kc)\), then average over the compact group \(H\), which preserves the trace.
  5. Fourier transform · eqs. (5.41)–(5.43)Evaluate the trace of the frame's projection.\(\tr(\one\otimes p)=\bet\int e^{-\bet\xi}\mU(\xi)\,d\xi\). Finite trace on the identity of the corner means a finite algebra.
  6. Takesaki duality · Prop D.9 · Cor 5.7Read off the type.For a III1 factor and trivial \(H\), the crossed product is a II∞ factor and its corner is II1 exactly when the trace of \(p\) is finite.
11Paper §5.2 · eqs. (5.44)–(5.48) · Thm 5.11

A thermal reading of the condition

If the frame's energy is bounded below by \(\xi_0\), let \(N(\xi)=\int_{\xi_0}^{\xi}\mU(\eta)\,d\eta\) count its degrees of freedom below energy \(\xi\). Integrating by parts, the condition implies \(\int_{\xi_0}^\infty e^{-\bet\xi}N(\xi)\,d\xi<\infty\): \(N\) must not grow too fast. This resembles the nuclearity conditions of QFT on Minkowski space, which bound how fast the number of localised states grows with energy, and which guarantee KMS states at positive temperature.

A frame cannot be in an ordinary equilibrium state. If \(\omega\) were invariant under time translations, then for the unit time bins \(X_n=[n,n+1)\) all the probabilities \(\omega(\E(X_n))\) would be equal. Since \(\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\E(X_n)\le\one\), each is at most \(1/(2N+1)\) for every \(N\), hence zero. No such state is normal.

What does exist is a normal weight \(\varphi_\beta\) on \(\Br(\HR)\), built from the same trace (eq. 5.60).

Theorem 5.11 The weight \(\varphi_\beta\) takes finite values on a dense *-subalgebra \(\mathcal D_1\subset\Br(\HR)\) if and only if \(\int e^{-\bet\xi}\mU(\xi)\,d\xi<\infty\). In that case it satisfies the KMS condition on \(\mathcal D_1\) at inverse temperature \(\bet\) with respect to \(\mathrm{Ad}\,\UR\).

In wordsType reduction happens when the frame can be in thermal equilibrium, in the weak sense of a KMS weight, at the same temperature as the field.

12Paper §6 · eqs. (6.6)–(6.25)

The de Sitter static patch

Take \(M\) to be the static patch, \(G=\mathbb R\times SO(n-1)\), and a QFT whose state is KMS for the static flow at some \(\bet\) and invariant under \(G\). Observables measured relative to a compactly stabilised frame lie in \(\inv{\MS\otimes\Br(\HR)}\), which is semifinite, and finite under condition (6.6).

Example 1a clock that ignores rotations
\(\HR=p_V L^2(\mathbb R)\), energies restricted to \(V\), with \(SO(n-1)\) acting trivially. Then \(\mU=\chi_V\) and the condition is \(\int_V e^{-\bet\xi}d\xi<\infty\), true whenever \(V\) is bounded below. CLPW's observer has \(V=[0,\infty)\), and they impose only time-translation invariance. If \(\MS\) is a type III1 factor, which holds for various QFTs on de Sitter, Corollary 5.7 gives a type II1 factor, in agreement with CLPW.
Example 2a frame that also tracks orientation, n = 4
\(\Sig=G\) and \(\HR\subset L^2(\mathbb R)\otimes L^2(SO(3))\). Taking all of \(L^2(SO(3))\) at every allowed energy gives \(\mU=\infty\cdot\chi_V\), and the condition fails. A physical rotor instead gives the angular-momentum sector \(l\) (multiplicity \((2l+1)^2\) by Peter–Weyl) an energy \(E_l\). Then \(\mU=\sum_l(2l+1)^2\chi_{[E_l,\infty)}\), and the condition becomes
\[ \sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)^2e^{-\bet E_l}<\infty, \]

which says precisely that \(e^{-\beta\hat H_{SO(3)}}\) is trace class: the rotor has a Gibbs state at the field's temperature. For a rigid rotor, \(E_l\propto l(l+1)\), this holds at every temperature. Both examples can be explored in Figure 7.

13Paper §7

What this does and does not show

In this account type reduction comes from including a reference frame and restricting to invariant observables. CLPW attributed it to the need to "gravitationally dress" observables. Here it is an operational consequence of measuring relative to a physical frame, and gravity enters only through the background's symmetries.

The authors do not take this as evidence that local algebras in QFT should stop being type III. The result relies on special features: nontrivial isometries, a modular flow that coincides with time translations, and suitable properties of the frame. General regions of curved spacetime have no isometries, and geometric modular action is rare. Type reduction may still happen in particular models with larger symmetry groups. The results say nothing directly about local algebras in quantum gravity. Related semifinite algebras in perturbative quantum gravity also rest on modular crossed products, with the assumption that some diffeomorphisms act modularly; the authors know of no concrete argument for that assumption.

Two idealisations are flagged in §4.1. Reading a continuous frame observable with perfect precision is impossible, so the random-variable picture is a limit. And ideally the reading of the frame and the measurement of the field would be one extended measurement scheme, which would require generalising the framework beyond compact coupling regions.

The paper in eight lines

  1. An isometry maps a measurement scheme to another scheme measuring the transformed observable (Thm 2.1); the two are indistinguishable without a reference.
  2. A quantum reference frame is a system with a covariant POVM on a homogeneous space \(\Sig\cong G/H\).
  3. Physical observables are the \(G\)-invariant elements of \(\MS\otimes\Br(\HR)\); relativisation \(\Yen\) produces many of them.
  4. To single out experiments with compact coupling regions, frames must be compactly stabilised (Prop 4.3).
  5. Such frames sit inside the regular representation (Thm 4.4), so the invariants are a corner of \((\MS\cp G)\otimes\Br(\Kc)\) (Thm 4.9).
  6. For \(G=\mathbb R\times H\) and a KMS state, Takesaki's trace makes the invariants semifinite, with \(\tr(\one)=\bet\int e^{-\bet\xi}\mU(\xi)\,d\xi\) (Thm 5.5).
  7. Finiteness is a growth condition on the frame's density of states, equivalent to a KMS weight at the field's temperature (Thm 5.11).
  8. On the de Sitter static patch this recovers CLPW's type II1 algebra and extends it to frames that also track rotations (§6).
