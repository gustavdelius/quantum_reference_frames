The Gap in AQFT

Algebras Without Apparatus

Haag–Kastler AQFT assigns a unital *-algebra A(M; N) to each causally convex open region N ⊂ M and declares self-adjoint elements to be "local observables". Yet standard AQFT never models how an observable is actually measured, nor what happens to the field state afterward.

The Gap in QMT & Relativistic Collapse

Operations Without Spacetime

Quantum Measurement Theory (Busch, Davies–Lewis, Ozawa) models measurements operationally by coupling a system to a probe, but usually in t-parameterized non-relativistic Hilbert space. Meanwhile, naive Lüders projection in QFT breaks Lorentz covariance or causes superluminal signaling (Sorkin's 1993 "impossible measurements").

Crucial Insight #1 (Page 860, Fig. 1)

Why M+ := M \ J−(K) instead of J+(K)?

When physicists first read M+ = M \ J−(K) ("Out") and M− = M \ J+(K) ("In"), they often wonder why "Out" isn't just J+(K). Look at the canvas on the left:

  • It contains full Cauchy surfaces S+ of M: J+(K) is spatially bounded (for compact K), so it never contains a Cauchy surface of M. By contrast, M+ = M \ J−(K) stretches all the way to spatial infinity (K⊥) and contains full Cauchy surfaces S+!
  • The Time-Slice Axiom applies to M+ and M−: Because M± contain Cauchy surfaces of M, the inclusion morphisms α± : A(M±) → A(M) and γ± : C(M±) → C(M) are algebraic isomorphisms (convertible in both directions!).
  • Physical Meaning of M− and M+: M− = M \ J+(K) is the set of all points that cannot have been influenced by the coupling K. M+ = M \ J−(K) is the set of all points that can no longer influence K.
  • They cover the exterior of the Causal Hull: Notice that M+ ∪ M− = M \ (J+(K) ∩ J−(K)), and their intersection is the causal complement M+ ∩ M− = M \ (J+(K) ∪ J−(K)) = K⊥!

Causal Hull J+(K) ∩ J−(K): All points lying on causal curves with both endpoints in K. A causal signal can leave K, travel through the gap inside J+(K) ∩ J−(K), and re-enter K! Therefore, in that gap, the coupled theory C already feels the interaction and cannot be identified with A ⊗ B.
The 5 AQFT Axioms (§2, p. 857):
  1. Isotony: N1 ⊂ N2 ⇒ A(M; N1) ⊂ A(M; N2)
  2. Compatibility: αM1;M2 ˆ αM2;M3 = αM1;M3
  3. Time-Slice: N contains Cauchy surface of M ⇒ A(M; N) = A(M)
  4. Einstein Causality: N1 ⊂ N2⊥ ⇒ [A(M; N1), A(M; N2)] = 0
  5. Haag Property (Weak Haag Duality): If A ∈ A(M) commutes with A(M; N) for every N ⊂ K⊥, then A ∈ A(M; L) for every connected open causally convex L ⊃ K (which automatically contains the causal hull of K!).

Step 1 / 6
Heisenberg-like View (Algebra Elements)

Why Θ = (τ−)−1 ˆ τ+ Maps Future → Past

In AQFT, dynamics is encoded in the identification of local algebras at different times. An observable B ∈ B(M) measured on the probe at late times (M+) corresponds in the coupled experiment C(M) to:

B̃ := τ+(1 ⊗ B) ∈ C(M)   (Eq. 3.7)

To evaluate its expectation value in a state prepared at early times (M−), we must express B̃ in the early-time uncoupled language by applying (τ−)−1: (τ−)−1(B̃) = (τ−)−1 τ+(1 ⊗ B) = Θ(1 ⊗ B). Because of the interaction in K, Θ(1 ⊗ B) is no longer of the form 1 ⊗ B'—it is an entangled sum ∑ Ai ⊗ Bi in A(M) ⊗ B(M)!

Schrödinger-like View (States / Functionals)

How Early Product States ω ⊗ σ Evolve Forward

At early times (M−), the experimenter prepares the system in state ω and the probe in state σ with no initial correlations. Pushing ω ⊗ σ forward into the coupled theory via the adjoint (τ−)−1* gives the coupled state:

ω̰σ := (τ−)−1*(ω ⊗ σ)   (Eq. 3.6)

Pairing the state ω̰σ with the late-time probe observable B̃ yields Eq. (3.8): ω̰σ(B̃) = ((τ−)−1*(ω ⊗ σ))(τ+(1 ⊗ B)) = (ω ⊗ σ)(Θ(1 ⊗ B)) = (Θ*(ω ⊗ σ))(1 ⊗ B).

How a Probe Measurement Induces a System Observable (§3.2, Theorem 3.2)

The experimenter only touches the probe observable B ∈ B(M) at late times, getting expectation value ω̰σ(B̃). When can we interpret this number as the expectation value ω(A) of a system observable A ∈ A(M)?

Step A: Scatter 1 ⊗ B
Θ(1 ⊗ B) = ∑r Ar ⊗ Br

The scattering morphism Θ entangles the late-time probe observable 1 ⊗ B into a sum of tensor products of system and probe operators.

Step B: Evaluate Probe State via ησ
ησ(A ⊗ B) := σ(B) A

The conditional expectation ησ : A⊗B → A plugs in the known early probe preparation state σ, leaving an operator purely in A(M).

Step C: Induced System Observable
A = ϵσ(B) := (ησ ˆ Θ)(1 ⊗ B)

Satisfies ω(ϵσ(B)) = ω̰σ(B̃) for all system states ω! Completely positive, unital (ϵσ(1)=1), and *-preserving.

Decoding the 3 Sharpness Levels (pp. 862–864)

Why ϵσ(B)2 ≤ ϵσ(B2) Causes Two Layers of Unsharpness

Because ϵσ is a completely positive map (not an algebra homomorphism!), Appendix B (Eq. B.8) proves the Kadison–Schwarz inequality:

ϵσ(B*B) − ϵσ(B)*ϵσ(B) = ∑r,s [ σ(Br*Bs) − σ(Br*)σ(Bs) ] Ar*As ≥ 0
1. Probe Observable B (Sharpest): Suppose the experimenter measures a sharp projection-valued effect E(X)2 = E(X) on the probe.
2. Induced System Observable A = ϵσ(B) (Intermediate): Even if E(X) is a sharp projector, the induced system effect satisfies ϵσ(E(X))2 ≤ ϵσ(E(X)2) = ϵσ(E(X)). So a sharp probe PVM induces an unsharp system POVM (EVM)! Hypothetically measuring A = ϵσ(B) directly on the system has variance Var(A; ω) = ω(A2) − ω(A)2.
3. Actual Measurement of B̃ in Coupled Theory (Least Sharp): In reality we measure B̃ = τ+(1⊗B) in state ω̰σ. Since (B̃)2 = τ+(1⊗B2), its second moment is ω(ϵσ(B2)), NOT ω(ϵσ(B)2)! By Eq. (3.14):
Var(B̃; ω̰σ) = ω(ϵσ(B2)) − ω(A)2 ≥ ω(A2) − ω(A)2 = Var(A; ω)
Theorem 3.3 (Page 864)

Where is ϵσ(B) Localized?

Suppose the experimenter reads the probe B in Andromeda a million years after the interaction in K. Where is the induced system observable ϵσ(B) localized?

  • Always in any connected open causally convex neighborhood of K (i.e., the Causal Hull of K): Take any system observable A ∈ A(M; L) in L ⊂ K⊥. Since Θ acts trivially on K⊥, Θ(A ⊗ 1) = A ⊗ 1. Then Eq. (3.16) gives:
    [ϵσ(B), A] = ησ(Θ[1 ⊗ B, A ⊗ 1]) = 0
    Since ϵσ(B) commutes with every A in K⊥, the Haag Property guarantees ϵσ(B) ∈ A(M; O) for every connected open causally convex O ⊃ K!
  • What if B itself is localized in K⊥? Then Θ(1 ⊗ B) = 1 ⊗ B, so ϵσ(B) = σ(B)1 (Eq. 3.15)—a trivial multiple of the identity! You learn nothing about the system if the probe is measured outside J+(K).

Theorem 3.4 & Reeh–Schlieder (Pages 866–867)

Why There is No Collapse Surface in Spacetime

Conditioned on observing probe effect B, the updated system state is ω'(A) = Iσ(B)(ω)(A) / ω(ϵσ(B)), where Iσ(B)(ω)(A) = (ω⊗σ)(Θ(A⊗B)). What is ω'(A) for a system observable A ∈ A(M; K⊥) at spacelike separation from K?

For A ∈ A(M; K⊥):   ω'(A) = ω(A ϵσ(B))ω(ϵσ(B))   (Eq. 3.26)
  • Theorem 3.4: ω'(A) = ω(A) if and only if A is uncorrelated with ϵσ(B) in state ω.
  • Reeh–Schlieder Consequence (Eq. 3.27): In any state ω with the Reeh–Schlieder property (such as the vacuum!), every open region O ⊂ K⊥ has entangled correlations with ϵσ(B). Thus ω' differs from ω everywhere in spacetime—in K⊥ and even in the causal past J−(K)!
  • Physical Resolution: ω ↦ ω' is not a physical shockwave occurring along a constant-t slice or backward lightcone &partial;J−(K) (Hellwig–Kraus 1970). It is Bayesian conditioning (filtering) of the global state on the classical measurement record.
  • Why no faster-than-light signaling? If Alice does not communicate her outcome to Bob, Bob sees the non-selective state ω'ns(A) = Iσ(1)(ω)(A) = (Θ*(ω⊗σ))(A⊗1). For A ∈ A(M; K⊥), Θ(A⊗1) = A⊗1, so ω'ns(A) = ω(A) identically!
Theorem 3.5 & Corollary 3.6 (Pages 868–869)

The Central Consistency Theorem: Causal Factorization

Suppose two probes B1, B2 couple to the system in compact regions K1, K2 with K2 ∩ J−(K1) = ∅ (K2 does not precede K1). Provided the combined scattering morphism satisfies Bogoliubov causal factorization:

Θ̂ = Θ̂1 ˆ Θ̂2   (Eq. 3.28 — note Θ̂2 acts first on future observables!)

then the pre-instruments compose covariantly (Theorem 3.5):

Iσ2(B2) ˆ Iσ1(B1) = Iσ1⊗σ2(B1 ⊗ B2)   (Eq. 3.29)

And if K1 ⊥ K2 (causally disjoint), both causal orderings hold, so Iσ2(B2) ˆ Iσ1(B1) = Iσ1(B1) ˆ Iσ2(B2)! Updating the state in any Lorentz frame's order—or all at once—yields the exact same post-selected state ω''12 = ω'12!

Joint EVMs (§3.2, pp. 864–865)

Spacelike Probe Readings vs. Incompatible System Observables

Suppose Alice and Bob measure a single probe at spacelike separated regions in M+ after it coupled to the system in K. By Einstein causality on the probe, their probe EVMs E1, E2 commute and define a joint probe EVM E. However, the induced system EVMs ϵσ ˆ E1 and ϵσ ˆ E2 are both localized in the causal hull of K and generally do not commute! How is this consistent? Because ϵσ ˆ E is an unsharp joint EVM: incompatible system observables cannot be measured jointly and sharply, but they can be measured jointly and unsharply!

Gauge Symmetries (§3.4, Theorem 3.7)

How Gauge Invariance Passes from Probe to System

If a global gauge group G acts on A, B, C and the coupling is gauge-invariant, then Θ commutes with φM(g) ⊗ ψM(g) and:

φ(g)(ϵσ(B)) = ϵψ(g−1)*σ(ψ(g)B)  (Eq. 3.42)

Thus, if σ is gauge-invariant, gauge-invariant probe observables can only measure gauge-invariant system observables! Measuring a gauge-noninvariant quantity requires a symmetry-breaking probe state σ or coupling.

Geometrical Symmetries (§3.4, Eq. 3.44–3.45)

Strong Mixing Erases Post-Selection Over Time

If the system state ω has the strong mixing (timelike clustering) property under time translations νs, ω((νsA)B) → ω(νsA)ω(B) as |s| → ∞, then:

ω'(νsA) − ω(νsA) = ω((νsA)ϵσ(B))ω(ϵσ(B)) − ω(νsA) → 0

Wait long enough after a measurement and ω' becomes indistinguishable from ω—allowing repeated experiments without active state re-preparation!
