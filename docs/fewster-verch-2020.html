Measuring a quantum field with a quantum field

Algebraic QFT assigns observables to regions of spacetime but says nothing about how anyone would measure them. Fewster and Verch close that gap. Couple the field you care about, the system, to a second field, the probe, inside a bounded region K. Read the probe afterwards. Then ask what you have learned about the system and what has happened to its state.

This page rebuilds their answer with diagrams you can drag. It assumes you know what a state on a *-algebra is and have met the measurement problem. Hover over any coloured symbol or dotted term for its definition; the Notation button lists them all. Section, equation and theorem numbers refer to the paper.

01Paper §1

The gap being closed

In algebraic QFT a theory is a net of *-algebras. Each open, causally convex region \(N\) of a spacetime \(M\) gets an algebra \(\cA(M;N)\), and its self-adjoint elements are read as the observables that can be measured in \(N\). The axioms constrain how these algebras fit together. They do not say which experiment measures a given element, or what a measurement does to the state. Haag and Kastler themselves preferred to read local elements as operations performed in \(N\) rather than as observables.

Quantum measurement theory has the opposite profile. It explains in detail how to measure an observable of a system: couple it to a probe, measure the probe, and infer. But it works with Hilbert spaces and has no spacetime in it. Fewster and Verch build the spacetime version, for QFT on any globally hyperbolic spacetime, with no particular interaction assumed.

What the paper does not try to do

It does not solve the measurement problem. It takes for granted that the experimenter can prepare, control and read the probe. The probe is itself a quantum field, and whatever happens when it is read lies outside the analysis. The paper studies one link in von Neumann's measurement chain, the link from the system field to the probe field.

The question is then concrete: what does a probe reading tell you about the system, and what does it do to the system's state? The framing still bears on the measurement problem. Hellwig and Kraus (1970) asked where in spacetime a relativistic state reduction should take place, and proposed the backward light cone of the measurement. Section 10 shows why that question loses its force here.

Causal geometry

Every construction in the paper is defined from the causal structure of a globally hyperbolic spacetime \(M\), never from a time coordinate. Five notions carry most of the weight. Figure 1 shows each of them for a coupling region \(\K\) of your choice.

Causal future and past\(\Jp(S)\), \(\Jm(S)\)
Everything reachable from \(S\) along future-directed (past-directed) timelike or null curves, \(S\) included.
Causal hull\(\Jp(S)\cap\Jm(S)\)
Every point on some causal curve that starts and ends in \(S\). A set equal to its causal hull is causally convex. Local algebras are attached to open causally convex regions.
Causal complement\(S^\perp = M\setminus(\Jp(S)\cup\Jm(S))\)
Everything that no causal curve connects to \(S\). Sets in each other's causal complement are causally disjoint.
Cauchy surface\(\Sigma\)
A set met exactly once by every inextendible timelike curve. Data on one Cauchy surface determine everything; this is what the time-slice axiom will encode.
Out- and in-regions\(M^{\pm} = M\setminus J^{\mp}(\K)\)
\(\Mp\) is everything not in the causal past of \(\K\), so nothing in it can influence \(\K\). \(\Mm\) is everything not in the causal future of \(\K\). Each contains Cauchy surfaces, and \(\Mp\cap\Mm = \Kp\).
03Paper §2

What AQFT provides

Fewster and Verch assume five properties of every theory in play: system, probe, and the coupled theory. Each one is used at a specific point in the argument.

Isotony
\(N_1\subset N_2 \Rightarrow \cA(M;N_1)\subset\cA(M;N_2)\). An observable has many localisation regions.
Compatibility
A region \(N\), viewed as a spacetime in its own right, has its own algebra \(\cA(N)\), embedded by an injective morphism \(\alpha_{M;N}:\cA(N)\to\cA(M)\) with image \(\cA(M;N)\), consistently for nested regions. Used forthe \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) arrows in Figure 2.
Time-slice
If \(N\) contains a Cauchy surface of \(M\), then \(\cA(M;N)=\cA(M)\). This is dynamics, stated algebraically. Used forthe in- and out-regions each carry the whole theory.
Einstein causality
Elements of algebras of causally disjoint regions commute. Used forprobe readings at spacelike separation are compatible.
Haag property
If \(A\) commutes with \(\cA(M;N)\) for every region \(N\subset\Kp\), then \(A\in\cA(M;L)\) for every connected open causally convex \(L\supset\K\). A weak form of Haag duality. Used forlocalising induced observables, Theorem 3.3.

States, effects and effect-valued measures

A state is a linear functional \(\om\) with \(\om(A^*A)\ge 0\) and \(\om(\one)=1\); \(\om(A)\) is the expected outcome of measuring \(A\). Measurement theory needs more than self-adjoint elements. An effect is an element \(E\) with \(0\le E\le\one\), and \(\om(E)\) is a probability. An effect-valued measure (EVM; a POVM in Hilbert-space language) assigns an effect \(E(X)\) to each set \(X\) of outcomes, additively, with \(E(\Omega)=\one\). Projection-valued EVMs are sharp, the rest unsharp. Unsharp observables are what real probes deliver, and the paper explains why.

The blueprint from quantum mechanics

In the standard model of a quantum measurement, a system in state \(\varrho\) meets a probe prepared in \(\sg\). They evolve together by a unitary \(U\), and a probe observable \(B\) is read. The expected reading is \(\mathrm{tr}\bigl[(\varrho\otimes\sg)\,U^*(\one\otimes B)U\bigr]\). Read in the Heisenberg picture, this identifies a system observable

\[ \varepsilon_\sigma(B) = (\mathrm{id}\otimes\sg)\bigl(U^*(\one\otimes B)\,U\bigr) \]

whose expectation in \(\varrho\) reproduces the probe statistics. Fewster and Verch keep this skeleton and replace each part with a local, covariant counterpart.

System, probe and their coupling

Four theories appear. \(\cA\) is the system and \(\cB\) the probe. \(\cU=\cA\otimes\cB\) is both of them with no interaction: the control experiment. \(\cC\) is the actual experiment, in which they interact.

The paper never specifies the interaction. It assumes only that \(\cC\) looks exactly like \(\cU\) away from the coupling. For each open causally convex \(L\) outside the causal hull of \(\K\) there is an isomorphism

\[ \chi_L:\cA(L)\otimes\cB(L)\to\cC(L), \]

compatible with the embeddings of smaller regions into larger ones (diagram (3.2)). Inside the causal hull, \(\cC\) may be anything at all.

The authors phrase this as a controlled experiment. You compare what happened with what would have happened without the interaction. That comparison, between the coupled and the uncoupled dynamics, is the source of everything that follows.

Running example · §4

Two real scalar fields on \(M\): the system \(\Ph\) and the probe \(\Ps\). The action is

\[ S=\tfrac12\int_M\!\bigl[(\nabla\Ph)^2-m_\Phi^2\Ph^2+(\nabla\Ps)^2-m_\Psi^2\Ps^2\bigr]d\mathrm{vol}\;-\;\int_M\!\rh\,\Ph\Ps\;d\mathrm{vol}, \]

with \(\rh\) smooth, real and supported in \(\K\). The field equations are

\[ T\begin{pmatrix}\Ph\\ \Ps\end{pmatrix}=0,\qquad T=\begin{pmatrix}P&R\\ R&Q\end{pmatrix},\quad P=\Box+m_\Phi^2,\quad Q=\Box+m_\Psi^2,\quad R=\rh\,\cdot\,. \]

For weak enough coupling \(T\) is Green-hyperbolic: it has an advanced Green operator \(\ETm\), whose solutions are supported in the causal past \(\Jm\) of the source, and a retarded one \(E_T^+\) with support in \(\Jp\). Each theory is quantised in the standard way, with smeared fields obeying \([\Ph(f),\Ph(g)]=i\EP(f,g)\one\) and \(\EP=E_P^- - E_P^+\). The point of the example is that every abstract object below can be computed. Figures 3 and 5 compute them on a 1+1-dimensional lattice.

The scattering morphism

In the out-region the coupled and uncoupled theories coincide, through \(\chi^+\). Since \(\Mp\) contains a Cauchy surface, the time-slice property extends this to an isomorphism \(\tp:\cU(M)\to\cC(M)\). It identifies an uncoupled observable with the coupled observable that agrees with it at late times. The in-region gives \(\tm\) in the same way, agreeing at early times. Comparing the two gives the scattering morphism

\[ \Th=(\tm)^{-1}\circ\tp\;:\;\cA(M)\otimes\cB(M)\to\cA(M)\otimes\cB(M). \]

\(\Th\) takes the late-time description of a coupled observable and returns its early-time description. It plays the part of \(B\mapsto U^*BU\). Figure 2 follows a single probe reading through every map involved.

Proposition 3.1 (properties of \(\Th\))
  1. Enlarging \(\K\) to any compact \(\hat K\supseteq\K\) gives the same \(\Th\).
  2. \(\Th\) acts trivially on \(\cU(M;L)\) for every open causally convex \(L\subset\Kp\).
  3. If \(L^\pm\subset M^\pm\) are open and causally convex with \(L^+\subset D(L^-)\), then \(\Th\,\cU(M;L^+)\subset\cU(M;L^-)\).

In words\(\Th\) belongs to the experiment, not to how you drew \(\K\). The coupling is invisible from regions causally disjoint from it. And \(\Th\) carries observables of a late region back to any early region whose Cauchy development contains it.

In the model · eq. (4.11), App. D

For \(F\in C_0^\infty(\Mp;\mathbb C^2)\), with \(\Xi_0(f\oplus h)=\Ph(f)\otimes\one+\one\otimes\Ps(h)\):

\[ \Th\,\Xi_0(F)=\Xi_0\bigl(F-\Rt\,\ETm F\bigr),\qquad \Rt=T-P\oplus Q=\begin{pmatrix}0&R\\ R&0\end{pmatrix}. \]

Induced system observables

The construction of Figure 2 defines, for each probe preparation \(\sg\), a map from probe observables to system observables:

\[ \es(B)=\ets\bigl(\Th(\one\otimes B)\bigr),\qquad \ets(A\otimes B)=\sg(B)\,A. \]
Theorem 3.2 For each probe preparation \(\sg\), \(A=\es(B)\) is the unique solution of \(\omt(\Bt)=\om(A)\) for all states \(\om\) (given that states separate \(\cA(M)\)). The map \(\es\) is linear and completely positive, with \[ \es(\one)=\one,\qquad \es(B^*)=\es(B)^*,\qquad \es(B)^*\es(B)\le\es(B^*B). \]

In words\(\es\) is a unital quantum channel in the Heisenberg picture, from probe to system. It is neither injective nor a homomorphism: different probe observables can induce the same system observable, and products are not preserved. Probe and coupling together form a measurement scheme for the induced observable.

In the model · eqs. (5.2), (5.4), (5.5)

For a probe test function \(h\) supported in \(\Mp\),

\[ \es(\Ps(h))=\Ph(\fm)+\sg(\Ps(\hm))\,\one,\qquad \begin{pmatrix}\fm\\ \hm\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\ h\end{pmatrix}-\Rt\,\ETm\begin{pmatrix}0\\ h\end{pmatrix}. \]

So \(\fm=-\rh\,\bigl(\ETm(0\oplus h)\bigr)_2\). Propagate the readout \(h\) backwards with the coupled advanced Green operator, keep the probe component, and multiply by \(\rh\). The result is supported in \(\operatorname{supp}\rh\cap\Jm(\operatorname{supp}h)\). As formal power series, \(\es(e^{i\Ps(h)})=\sg(e^{i\Ps(\hm)})\,e^{i\Ph(\fm)}\).

Why the readout is noisier

Because \(\es\) is completely positive but not a homomorphism, the Kadison–Schwarz inequality \(\es(B)^2\le\es(B^2)\) holds for self-adjoint \(B\). The map \(\tp\) is a homomorphism, so \(\Bt^{\,2}=\widetilde{B^2}\). Taking expectations gives

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\Bt;\omt)=\om(\es(B^2))-\om(\es(B))^2\;\ge\;\operatorname{Var}(\es(B);\om). \]

The actual readings scatter at least as much as the induced observable would if you could measure it directly. The excess comes from the probe's own quantum fluctuations.

Sharpness degrades in the same way. If \(E\) is a sharp probe EVM, the induced system EVM \(\es\circ E\) satisfies only \(\es(E(X))^2\le\es(E(X))\), so in general it is unsharp. The induced observable is less sharp than the probe observable, but sharper than the measurement actually made on the coupled system.

The model makes this exact. The variances add,

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\widetilde{\Ps(h)};\omt)=\operatorname{Var}(\Ph(\fm);\om)+\operatorname{Var}(\Ps(\hm);\sg), \]

and the distribution of readings is the convolution of the distribution of \(\Ph(\fm)\) in \(\om\) with that of \(\Ps(\hm)\) in \(\sg\). Figure 4 shows this for Gaussian (quasi-free) states.

Where an induced observable lives

Theorem 3.3 For each probe observable \(B\in\cB(M)\), the induced system observable \(\es(B)\) may be localised in any connected open causally convex set containing \(\K\). If \(B\) may be localised in \(\Kp\), then \(\es(B)=\sg(B)\one\).

Proof ideaFor \(A\) localised in \(L\subset\Kp\), Proposition 3.1(b) says \(\Th\) fixes \(A\otimes\one\). So \([\es(B),A]=\ets\bigl(\Th[\one\otimes B,A\otimes\one]\bigr)=0\). Thus \(\es(B)\) commutes with everything in \(\Kp\), and the Haag property places it in every connected causally convex neighbourhood of \(\K\). If \(B\) itself is localised in \(\Kp\), then \(\Th\) fixes \(\one\otimes B\) and \(\ets\) returns \(\sg(B)\one\).

Notice what the theorem does not depend on: where the probe is read. The readout region can be far away in space and in time. The information about the system was imprinted on the probe in \(\K\), and that is where the induced observable lives.

Why not something smaller?

In the model, \(\es(\Ps(h))=\Ph(\fm)+\text{const}\) with \(\fm\) supported in \(\operatorname{supp}\rh\). It is tempting to localise it in \(\operatorname{supp}\fm\). The paper argues against this. Whether two induced observables are compatible is decided by

\[ [\es(\Ps(h)),\es(\Ps(h'))]=[\Ph(\fm),\Ph(\fpm)]=i\EP(\fm,\fpm)\one, \]

and this number is sensitive to the spacetime geometry throughout the region (5.23) spanned by causal curves between the two supports. That region can contain points outside \(\operatorname{supp}\fm\), and even outside \(\operatorname{supp}\rh\). A localisation region that ignores geometry the observable responds to would mislead, and it would clash with local covariance. The causal hull of the coupling region is the smallest useful common localisation region. For a coupling along a timelike curve \(\gamma:[0,\tau]\to M\) it is \(\Jp(\gamma(0))\cap\Jm(\gamma(\tau))\); for an eternally accelerated detector it is a whole Rindler wedge (Figure 8).

Two experimenters, one probe

Suppose two experimenters read the probe in causally disjoint regions. Einstein causality makes their probe observables commute, so they can be measured jointly. Their induced system observables both live near \(\K\), and nothing forces those to commute. The commutator above is generally nonzero.

The paper's resolution: the joint probe EVM induces a joint EVM for the two system observables, and that joint EVM is unsharp. The experimenters keep the freedom to measure the probe however they like. Incompatible system observables still cannot be measured jointly and sharply; complementarity survives as unsharpness. In the model this shows up as a deformed product \(\star\) on \(\cB(M)\) for which \(\es\) becomes a homomorphism, with \([\Ps(h),\Ps(h')]_\star=i\EP(\fm,\fpm)\one\) (5.20). The interaction correlates degrees of freedom of the probe, and the correlations are then observed at spacelike separation.

Updating the state

Suppose the probe effect \(B\) is observed. A system effect \(A\), measured jointly with it at late times, has \(\mathrm{Prob}_\sigma(A\,\&\,B;\om)=\om\bigl(\ets\Th(A\otimes B)\bigr)\). Conditioning gives

\[ \mathrm{Prob}_\sigma(A\,|\,B;\om)=\frac{\Is(B)(\om)(A)}{\Is(B)(\om)(\one)},\qquad \Is(B)(\om)(A):=(\om\otimes\sg)\bigl(\Th(A\otimes B)\bigr). \]

\(\Is(B)\) is the pre-instrument, and the normalised state \(\om'=\Is(B)(\om)/\Is(B)(\om)(\one)\) is the post-selected state. Composing with a probe EVM \(E\) gives a Davies–Lewis instrument \(X\mapsto\Is(E(X))\).

Non-selectiveno outcome recorded
\(\om_{\rm ns}=\Is(\one)(\om)\): the partial trace of \(\Th^*(\om\otimes\sg)\) over the probe. It does not depend on what was measured. For \(A\) localised in \(\Kp\), \(\Th(A\otimes\one)=A\otimes\one\), so \(\om_{\rm ns}(A)=\om(A)\). Nobody in \(\Kp\) can tell, without being told the outcome, that a measurement took place. No signalling.
Selectiveoutcome recorded
For \(A\) localised in \(\Kp\): \(\displaystyle\om'(A)=\frac{\om(A\,\es(B))}{\om(\es(B))}\).
Theorem 3.4 If the probe effect \(B\) is observed, then for each \(A\in\cA(M;\Kp)\) the expectation value of \(A\) is unchanged in the post-selected state if and only if \(A\) is uncorrelated with \(\es(B)\) in \(\om\).

In states with the Reeh–Schlieder property, such as the vacuum, correlations reach every region. If \(\om'\) agreed with \(\om\) on all of \(\cA(M;O)\) for some region \(O\subset\Kp\), then \(\es(B)\) would be a multiple of \(\one\). So any informative probe measurement changes expectation values in every region causally disjoint from \(\K\). This is the field-theoretic version of an EPR experiment. Learning one spin of a singlet changes your predictions for the distant other spin, with nothing physical travelling there. If the system obeys Huygens' principle, the same argument reaches regions in the causal past of \(\K\).

No collapse surface

The post-selected state \(\om'\) differs from \(\om\) in no geometrically distinguished region. So there is nothing to be gained by picturing a transition from \(\om\) to \(\om'\) along some surface: not a constant-time surface, not the backward light cone proposed by Hellwig and Kraus, and not Schlieder's earlier proposal. As Hellwig and Kraus themselves recognised, whether the state changes in the past of \(\K\) is a convention without operational content, since that region is no longer open to experiment. It also does not matter when the probe is read. The updated state records the observer's conditioning on an outcome. It does not describe a physical process located in spacetime.

The circle back to Haag and Kastler closes here. In a C*-setting, for \(A\) localised in \(\Kp\) one can write \(\om'(A)=\om\bigl(\es(B)^{1/2}A\,\es(B)^{1/2}\bigr)/\om(\es(B))\). Seen from \(\Kp\), the post-selected state looks as if an operation in their sense had been performed, by an element \(\es(B)^{1/2}\) localisable near the causal hull of \(\K\). eq. (3.36)

Successive measurements

Now couple two probes, in regions \(K_1\) and \(K_2\), with \(K_2\cap\Jm(K_1)=\emptyset\). Then some Cauchy surface has \(K_1\) to its past and \(K_2\) to its future. Treat the two probes together as a single probe with algebra \(\cB_1(M)\otimes\cB_2(M)\) and coupling region \(K_1\cup K_2\). The key assumption is causal factorisation:

\[ \hat\Th=\hat\Th_1\circ\hat\Th_2. \]

Since \(\Th\) maps late descriptions to early ones, the later coupling is undone first. The formula is a special case of Bogoliubov's factorisation in perturbative AQFT, and Appendix D proves it for the scalar model.

Theorem 3.5 For all probe preparations \(\sigma_i\) and all probe observables \(B_i\), \[ \mathcal I_{\sigma_2}(B_2)\circ\mathcal I_{\sigma_1}(B_1)=\mathcal I_{\sigma_1\otimes\sigma_2}(B_1\otimes B_2). \] If \(K_1\) and \(K_2\) are causally disjoint, this also equals \(\mathcal I_{\sigma_1}(B_1)\circ\mathcal I_{\sigma_2}(B_2)\).

Corollary 3.6Post-selecting on \(B_1\) and then on \(B_2\) gives the same state as post-selecting on \(B_1\otimes B_2\) at once.

An experiment can be analysed as a sequence of measurements in any order compatible with causality, or as one combined measurement, with the same predictions. Experimenters in causally disjoint regions can work in ignorance of each other, or pool their results afterwards. No step reduces the state across a hypersurface. The authors call this the central consistency property of the framework, and the core result of the paper.

Mathematically, pre-instruments carry a monoidal structure that is symmetric for causally disjoint regions. For strictly causally ordered regions the symmetry breaks, which a Euclidean formulation could not see. Bostelmann, Fewster and Ruep build on this theorem in their analysis of Sorkin's “impossible measurements” [8].

Symmetries

Gauge symmetryThm 3.7
Let a global gauge group \(G\) act on \(\cA\), \(\cB\) and \(\cC\), with a gauge-invariant coupling. Then \(\Th\) commutes with the gauge action, and \(\varphi(g)(\es(B))=\varepsilon_{\psi(g^{-1})^*\sigma}(\psi(g)B)\). If \(\sg\) is gauge invariant, gauge-invariant probe observables induce gauge-invariant system observables. To see a gauge-variant quantity you need a probe or coupling that breaks the symmetry, as when an external field reveals the magnetisation of a spin chain. This is the natural entry point for quantum reference frames, developed for spacetime symmetries by Fewster, Janssen, Loveridge, Rejzner and Waldron (2024).
Time translationseq. (3.45)
If \(\om\) is strongly mixing under time translations \(\nu_s\), then \(\om'(\nu_sA)-\om(\nu_sA)\to0\) as \(|s|\to\infty\). The effect of post-selection fades with time, so an experiment can be repeated without re-preparing the system. Ground states of typical Minkowski QFTs are strongly mixing.

Detectors and the Unruh effect

Replace \(\rh\) by \(\lambda\rh\) with \(|\lambda|\) small. A Born expansion of \(\ETm\) gives

\[ \fm=-\lambda\rh E_Q^-h+O(\lambda^3),\qquad \hm=h+\lambda^2\rh E_P^-\rh E_Q^-h+O(\lambda^4). \]

Suppose \(\sg\) has vanishing one-point function and \(\Ps(h)\) is an annihilation operator for one mode of the probe's Fock representation. Then the induced number operator has expectation \(\om(\es(N_h))=\lambda^2W(h_1,h_1)+O(\lambda^4)\), with \(h_1=\rh E_Q^-h\) and \(W\) the two-point function of \(\om\). Concentrate \(\rh\) on a worldline \(\gamma(\tau)\) with switching function \(\tilde\rho(\tau)\), and let the mode oscillate as \(e^{iE\tau}\) along it:

\[ \om(\es(N_h))=\lambda^2\!\int\!d\tau\,d\tau'\;e^{-iE(\tau-\tau')}\,\tilde\rho(\tau)\tilde\rho(\tau')\,W\bigl(\gamma(\tau),\gamma(\tau')\bigr)+O(\lambda^4). \]

The coefficient of \(\lambda^2\) is the first-order response function of a switched Unruh–DeWitt detector with energy gap \(E\). The familiar detector calculation is recovered, now with a statement of which field observable the detector measures and where that observable is localised.

An earlier paper by Fewster, Juárez-Aubry and Louko was titled “Waiting for Unruh”. The framework says where to wait: in \(\Mp\cap\Jp(\K)\).

14Paper §6

The paper in eight lines

  1. Couple system \(\cA\) and probe \(\cB\) in a compact region \(\K\). Assume only that the coupled theory \(\cC\) matches \(\cA\otimes\cB\) outside the causal hull of \(\K\).
  2. \(\K\) fixes the in- and out-regions \(M^\mp\) covariantly. The time-slice property gives \(\tau^\pm\), and \(\Th=(\tm)^{-1}\circ\tp\).
  3. \(\es(B)=\ets(\Th(\one\otimes B))\) is the system observable the probe measures: unique, completely positive, unital.
  4. Readings are noisier than \(\es(B)\), and sharp probe questions become unsharp system questions.
  5. \(\es(B)\) is localised near \(\K\) wherever the probe is read, and trivial if \(B\) is localised in \(\Kp\).
  6. The state update is the instrument \(\Is(B)\). Non-selective updates leave \(\Kp\) untouched; selective ones change it through correlations.
  7. Given causal factorisation, instruments compose in every causally admissible order with the same result.
  8. The scalar model makes all of this explicit and reproduces the Unruh–DeWitt response function.

Left open by the authors: the converse problem (which system observables some probe can measure), measurements that act back on the spacetime, and a C*-algebraic version of the model in which the effects of Section 8 exist as genuine algebra elements.
